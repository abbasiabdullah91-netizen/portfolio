The problem

A hospitality group, 15+ brands. Every location collects public reviews. Replies were manual, so they were inconsistent, and an unanswered complaint is the first thing a customer reads before choosing where to eat.

What we delivered

An AI system that drafts and publishes brand-voiced replies to Google reviews across the group, twice a day, with one category permanently reserved for a human: food safety, legal disputes, and anything touching safety or harm. I set the workflow and acceptance criteria; technical partners implemented it.

How it works

The system checks for new reviews. Each one goes to an AI model carrying the brand's approved reply rules: short, acknowledge, thank or apologise, close, no names, no emojis. The same model sorts the review into ordinary or sensitive. Ordinary replies publish themselves. Sensitive ones go to a person.

What it proves

  1. I diagnose before we build. An eight-week publishing failure had been attributed to the platform. It was five stacked operational faults: expired balances, full storage, two formatting defects. The AI was never the problem, and finding that saved a rebuild.
  2. I decided where the human stays. Removing the last approval step was possible. I refused it for the sensitive category. A machine that auto-replies to a food-safety complaint is a brand risk no efficiency saving pays for.
  3. I learned what our controls could not see. Our monitoring initially detected stoppages but missed excess activity. One listing over-replied for days while every check read green, and a person noticing the volume felt wrong was what caught it. We added the anomaly check afterwards.

The problem

Customer feedback arrived across several platforms and was read by nobody in any structured way. Leadership had a star average and nothing else. "Service is slipping" was something you heard from a manager, weeks late.

What we delivered

A customer-sentiment pilot that scores feedback by theme and flags serious complaints. It collects reviews and comments nightly, scores them by theme, and puts them on a leadership screen, with an immediate alert path. Wider rollout remains gated. I set the workflow and acceptance criteria; technical partners implemented it.

How the scoring works

Each night the system collects new feedback. An AI model breaks every review into the things the customer actually mentioned, food, service, speed, cleanliness, price and more, and scores each one separately. A review that praises the food and slates the wait counts as one positive and one negative, not one lukewarm. During the pilot measurement, serious complaints were flagged the same day at one flagship outlet.

What it proves

  1. The unit of measurement was the decision that mattered. Scoring per review would have averaged away the exact thing an operator needs. Scoring per theme is why the output says "speed of service, this location, worsening" instead of "sentiment down slightly".
  2. I refused a comparison I could not defend. Leadership wanted a month-on-month trend. I held it, because the earlier months carried fewer data sources than the later ones, so the chart would have shown a change in data collection as a change in customer sentiment. A screen with no trend line is weaker, and I said so, rather than ship a flattering comparison.
  3. I caught a number with the wrong sign. Two defensible ways of averaging the same data gave opposite answers. One of them would have put a negative score on the boardroom screen for a month that was net positive. A metric can be arithmetically correct and still be the wrong metric.

The problem

The group was about to renew a paid analytics subscription for data the platforms already give away free through their own interface. Worse, the numbers it produced did not reconcile against what finance said had been spent, so the dashboard and the ledger disagreed and nobody could say which one was wrong.

What we delivered

An owned reporting pipeline. The pipeline refreshes reporting for participating social accounts nightly, pulling organic and paid performance directly from the platform and regenerating one leadership dashboard. No vendor in the middle. I set the workflow and acceptance criteria; technical partners implemented it.

What runs each night

The server asks the platform for yesterday's numbers, stores them, and rebuilds the page. The page displays its data date and coverage gaps; runs can fail. Leadership opens one link.

What it proves

  1. Buy or own is a judgment, not a policy. The subscription bought convenience over a free interface. At this volume, owning it removed a vendor sitting between the business and its own performance data.
  2. I held the spend figure back until it reconciled. The spend figure did not go live until it tied exactly to the finance-verified total. We reconcile against available finance-verified totals. Unverified periods are identified; affected breakdowns are withheld when checks fail.
  3. I state what the data cannot do. Some figures the platform simply will not give you. Where that is true, the number sits beside the headline instead of being folded into it, and the dashboard says so. Two of the worst defects I caught were two numbers that looked alike being added together.

How I work

Three rules, and what each one cost me to learn.

  1. Diagnose before building. I stopped a rebuild after finding that the assumed cause was wrong. I look for the real fault first, even when a plausible one is already agreed.
  2. Decide where the human stays. Full automation was available in the review-reply system. I keep the approval step where the failure mode is a brand risk no efficiency saving pays for.
  3. Reconcile against verified totals where available. We reconcile against available finance-verified totals; unverified periods are identified, and affected breakdowns are withheld when checks fail.
  4. How errors get caughtI use a shared written record and independent checks to reduce errors, including review by a model from a different vendor, because a second copy of the same engine shares its blind spots.
