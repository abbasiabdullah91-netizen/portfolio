Review replies across a hospitality group

Replies went from whenever someone had time to twice a day. The risky ones stayed with a person.

  • Review coverage widened roughly 40% in a single evening.
  • Total running cost is a fraction of one junior hire.
  • Food safety, legal and anything touching harm still goes to a person.
Read the build

The problem

A hospitality group, 15+ brands. Every location collects public reviews. Replies were manual, so they were inconsistent, and an unanswered complaint is the first thing a customer reads before choosing where to eat.

What I built

An AI system that drafts and publishes brand-voiced replies to Google reviews across the group, twice a day, with one category permanently reserved for a human: food safety, legal disputes, and anything touching safety or harm.

How it works

The system checks for new reviews. Each one goes to an AI model carrying the brand's approved reply rules: short, acknowledge, thank or apologise, close, no names, no emojis. The same model sorts the review into ordinary or sensitive. Ordinary replies publish themselves. Sensitive ones go to a person.

What it proves

  1. I diagnose before I build. An eight-week publishing failure had been attributed to the platform. It was five stacked operational faults: expired balances, full storage, two formatting defects. The AI was never the problem, and finding that saved a rebuild.
  2. I decided where the human stays. Removing the last approval step was possible. I refused it for the sensitive category. A machine that auto-replies to a food-safety complaint is a brand risk no efficiency saving pays for.
  3. I learned what my own controls could not see. Every monitor I built detected failure. None detected too much. One listing over-replied for days while every check read green, and a person noticing the volume felt wrong was what caught it. I built the anomaly check afterwards.

Owned analytics in place of a paid subscription

Leadership reporting at roughly 3% of the subscription it replaced, and a spend figure that finally tied to the finance total.

  • Runs at roughly 3% of the running cost of the subscription it replaced.
  • The spend figure went live only once it tied to the finance-verified total.
Read the build

The problem

The group was about to renew a paid analytics subscription for data the platforms already give away free through their own interface. Worse, the numbers it produced did not reconcile against what finance said had been spent, so the dashboard and the ledger disagreed and nobody could say which one was wrong.

What I built

An owned reporting pipeline. A small server pulls organic and paid performance for every brand handle directly from the platform each night and regenerates one leadership dashboard. No vendor in the middle.

What runs each night

The server asks the platform for yesterday's numbers, stores them, and rebuilds the page. If a run fails, the page shows its own age, so an old number can never pass as a fresh one. Leadership opens one link.

What it proves

  1. Buy or own is a judgment, not a policy. The subscription bought convenience over a free interface. At this volume, owning it cost a fraction and removed a vendor sitting between the business and its own performance data.
  2. I held the spend figure back until it reconciled. The spend figure did not go live until it tied exactly to the finance-verified total. That check runs inside the job and stops it rather than publishing something plausible and wrong.
  3. I state what the data cannot do. Some figures the platform simply will not give you. Where that is true, the number sits beside the headline instead of being folded into it, and the dashboard says so. Two of the worst defects I caught were two numbers that looked alike being added together.

Theme-level customer sentiment

One blended star average became eleven themes, scored separately. An operator can now see which thing is slipping, and where.

  • 11 themes scored separately, in place of one blended star average.
  • Serious complaints flagged the same day instead of buried in the pile.
Read the build, and try the scorer

The problem

Customer feedback arrived across several platforms and was read by nobody in any structured way. Leadership had a star average and nothing else. "Service is slipping" was something you heard from a manager, weeks late.

What I built

A nightly customer-sentiment engine. It collects reviews and comments, scores them by theme, and puts them on a leadership screen, with an immediate alert path for serious complaints.

How the scoring works

Each night the system collects new feedback. An AI model breaks every review into the things the customer actually mentioned, food, service, speed, cleanliness, price and more, and scores each one separately. A review that praises the food and slates the wait counts as one positive and one negative, not one lukewarm. Anything touching safety, misconduct or fraud is flagged the same day.

What it proves

  1. The unit of measurement was the decision that mattered. Scoring per review would have averaged away the exact thing an operator needs. Scoring per theme is why the output says "speed of service, this location, worsening" instead of "sentiment down slightly".
  2. I refused a comparison I could not defend. Leadership wanted a month-on-month trend. I held it, because the earlier months carried fewer data sources than the later ones, so the chart would have shown a change in data collection as a change in customer sentiment. A screen with no trend line is weaker, and I said so, rather than ship a flattering comparison.
  3. I caught a number with the wrong sign. Two defensible ways of averaging the same data gave opposite answers. One of them would have put a negative score on the boardroom screen for a month that was net positive. A metric can be arithmetically correct and still be the wrong metric.

How I work with AI

I am not an engineer. The builds above exist because I run AI systems the way I ran commercial teams: set the brief, delegate, verify, ship.

  1. A managed team of models, not a chatbotOne model plans and delegates, others build in parallel, and a model from a different vendor reviews the work adversarially before anything ships — because a second copy of the same engine shares its blind spots.
  2. An operating system for the modelsA shared written memory every model reads from and writes back to, custom skills and routines for repeat jobs, and checks that catch a stale or invented fact before it spreads. It is how the systems above get built and maintained.
  3. Infrastructure by instructionI stand up new tooling by briefing an agent and verifying the result — calling one model from inside another, routing work between models, standing up an open-source tool end-to-end — rather than waiting on a developer.
  4. Scale through workflowsFor broad or repetitive jobs I design the workflow and let sub-agents fan out and report back, with the self-checks they must pass before the work reaches me.

How I work

Three rules, and what each one cost me to learn
  1. Diagnose before building. I stopped a rebuild after finding that the assumed cause was wrong. I look for the real fault first, even when a plausible one is already agreed.
  2. Decide where the human stays. Full automation was available in the review-reply system. I keep the approval step where the failure mode is a brand risk no efficiency saving pays for.
  3. A number ships when it reconciles. A figure goes live once it ties to an independently verified total. The check sits inside the job and stops it rather than publishing something plausible and wrong.
